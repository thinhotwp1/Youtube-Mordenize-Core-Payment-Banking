===== Slide 1 · Overnight Engine. Real-Time World.  (00:00 – 02:50) =====

[tag ①]
This series uses a payments sandbox to examine a challenge I know from large enterprise systems: how do you change a critical platform while it keeps running?
The methods apply beyond banking.
I will show how confirmed business needs become architecture, code, tests, release controls, and operating decisions.
In this reference scenario, COBOL and CICS process payments, DB2 holds balances, and a nightly batch completes some work.
Bank specialists own the payment rules and obligations; an actual programme would confirm its estate and requirements before design.

[tag ②]
First, business opportunity.
A bank may want faster payment products, but the target depends on its market, scheme, and customers.
The architecture must support the product decision the bank actually makes.

[tag ③]
Second, cost.
Mainframe usage can be expensive, but the saving from moving a workload is not automatic.
We need a measured baseline, a cost model, and proof that old capacity can really be removed.

[tag ④]
Third, change risk.
Legacy knowledge may sit with a few people, and release cycles can be slow.
We must discover the hidden rules before replacing behavior.

[tag ⑤]
A single cutover concentrates too much risk in one moment.
The safer starting point is to identify a small, reversible slice, define its failure modes, and agree what evidence is required before more traffic moves.
That is an engineering proposal, not a claim that every bank should follow the same sequence.

[tag ⑥]
One useful pattern is the Strangler Fig: route one well-defined flow through a new path while the old one still runs.
Its engineering lessons can transfer from other large systems, but payment semantics do not map one-to-one.
Bank specialists must define the flow and its controls.

[tag ⑦]
We then measure outcomes the bank selects: customer impact, release frequency, cost, and operational risk.
Targets come from its baseline and risk appetite.
Throughout the series, I will show the decisions and the evidence I would put in front of a leadership team.

[validation plan]
Versioned code, contracts, and tests would provide engineering evidence for the reference design.
The proposed checks here are not evidence that this design is running in a bank.

===== Slide 2 · What "Done" Looks Like  (02:50 – 05:40) =====

[tag ①]
Before design, the bank and engineering team must agree what "done" means.
For this reference scenario, assume twelve million payments a day and a possible peak of five thousand per second.
These are planning inputs, not observed bank volumes.
A real programme would replace them with measured traffic, seasonal peaks, and growth forecasts.

[tag ②]
What must the flow do?
This diagram shows intake, checks, funds control, network submission, and customer status.
The bank's product, scheme, fraud, sanctions, and operations experts must define the actual order, messages, and exception rules.
ISO 20022 is a message standard, not a substitute for those decisions.

[tag ③]
How well must it work?
This example uses a p99 internal response target of half a second, an end-to-end target of ten seconds, and a ninety-nine point nine nine percent availability target.
These numbers are proposed targets for discussion.
The bank must validate them against its scheme rules, customer promise, cost, and operating capacity.

[tag ④]
Recovery also needs explicit RPO and RTO targets: how much data loss is tolerable, and how long recovery may take.
Zero data loss and fifteen minutes are useful design challenges here.
They are not guarantees; tests must prove the chosen design.

[tag ⑤]
Idempotency is a critical engineering control.
A client may retry after a timeout.
The platform must not create a second debit for the same authorised request.
The bank must define when two requests count as the same payment, and operations needs a way to investigate uncertain outcomes.

[tag ⑥]
Team design follows ownership.
In this example, delivery squads own bounded parts of the flow, while a platform team owns shared infrastructure.
Bank product, operations, risk, and compliance owners remain accountable for the rules and approvals.
Engineering owns implementation and measurable reliability.

[tag ⑦]
Legacy specialists are essential.
They know which behavior is deliberate and which is historical.
Their time must be funded for discovery, review, and tests, alongside keeping the current system safe.

[validation plan]
A load test and retry test can validate the engineering approach in a reference environment.
Production targets still require bank data and production-like testing.

===== Slide 3 · Strangle, Don't Rewrite  (05:40 – 08:40) =====

[tag ①]
Here is a possible current-state design, not a description of a specific bank.
Channels feed IBM MQ, COBOL programs run in CICS, DB2 stores balances, and some outputs leave in a nightly batch.
The first task is to verify every interface and dependency.
In large systems, hidden dependencies make even a small change risky.

[tag ②]
Step one is routing.
A gateway authenticates callers and carries an idempotency key.
A router chooses the old or new path using controlled flags.
The bank would approve the routing criteria, such as product or pilot group, and the fallback conditions.
A flag can reverse traffic only while state and external effects remain safe to reverse; it is not a promise of one-click rollback.

[tag ③]
Step two is decoupling.
Amazon MSK, managed Kafka, can carry versioned events between services.
That gives us an audit-friendly history and reduces direct coupling, but it also creates delivery, ordering, and replay questions.
Schemas, retention, access controls, and failure tests matter as much as the broker choice.

[tag ④]
In this option, services run on Amazon EKS.
Initiation, orchestration, screening integration, and the scheme gateway have clear contracts and data ownership.
Separate databases may reduce coupling, but only if the team can operate the extra complexity.
The bank and engineering team should choose the boundary together.

[tag ⑤]
Step three is connecting old and new.
Change data capture, if the source supports it safely, can publish changes to the new path.
An adapter translates commands to the legacy interface.
We would measure lag, missing records, and retry behavior before trusting either link.
These mechanisms are hypotheses until the estate and data owners validate them.

[tag ⑥]
The ledger is the highest-risk boundary in this scenario.
One possible sequence moves orchestration first, builds a shadow ledger for comparison, and considers the system-of-record cutover only after evidence and bank sign-off.
The sequence may change once we understand the bank's accounting and operations.

[tag ⑦]
Resilience needs a tested design across zones and, where justified, regions.
Encryption and end-to-end tracing are design controls.
Recovery claims must come from drills and reconciliation, not a diagram.

[validation plan]
A reference build using Spring, Kafka, Postgres, Debezium, and simulated interfaces can test these contracts.
It does not prove the bank's production integration.

===== Slide 4 · Phase 2: Prove It Before We Trust It  (08:40 – 11:00) =====

[tag ①]
Phase two is about earning trust in the new path while the current ledger remains authoritative.
In this reference design, DB2 is the system of record.
The bank's finance and operations owners must define what that means in their actual system.
Mainframe cost can be measured, but a shadow path may add cost before it removes any.

[tag ②]
First, we propose copying relevant changes through CDC.
We must confirm source permissions, latency, completeness, and what happens when the feed stops.
A customer should see no difference only after those controls are proven.

[tag ③]
A proposed shadow ledger processes the same inputs without deciding customer balances.
It gives us a comparison path.
The bank's accounting specialists define the posting rules and acceptable differences.

[tag ④]
Reconciliation compares records, balances, and end-of-day totals at an agreed cadence.
Differences are classified, owned, and resolved.
Finance defines the tolerances; engineering makes the comparison repeatable.

[tag ⑤]
Some read workloads might move to the copy after data freshness and access rules are approved.
That may reduce mainframe work, but we would verify the actual savings against the extra cloud and dual-run cost.

[tag ⑥]
When the two paths disagree, we do not hide the difference.
We trace source data, timing, and rules, then replay after the bank approves a correction.
A mismatch may reveal a defect in either system or an assumption we got wrong.

[tag ⑦]
The exit gate needs evidence, not optimism: stable reconciliation across the bank's chosen cycles, peak and recovery tests, open-risk review, and sign-off from the bank's accountable owners.
The exact gate belongs to that bank.

[validation plan]
A Postgres and Debezium reference test can show how a CDC gap is detected.
A real DB2 integration needs separate validation with the bank's specialists.

===== Slide 5 · Phase 3: Flip Safely. Fail Gracefully.  (11:00 – 14:00) =====

[tag ①]
Phase three is a possible cutover, when the bank may approve a new system of record.
We would begin with a controlled group and grow only after agreed checks pass.
Customer segments here are illustrative.
A feature flag can redirect new traffic, but it cannot undo a settled external payment.
The rollback plan must distinguish reversible routing from irreversible business effects.

[tag ②]
The shadow ledger becomes authoritative only after the bank proves posting accuracy, operations, controls, and recovery, then formally approves the change.

[tag ③]
The old path may remain available for an agreed fallback period.
Decommissioning comes later, after reconciliation and dependency checks.
We would measure any cost reduction rather than assume it.

[tag ④]
How do we handle a flow across services and an external network?
A global two-phase commit is usually a poor fit: we cannot hold one transaction open across organisations, and locks would harm availability.
The engineering choice needs an explicit state model, retries, and compensating actions, with bank approval for uncertain outcomes.

[tag ⑤]
A saga is one option for coordinating local steps.
Some steps can be reversed; others need a new business action, not an automatic undo.
ISO 20022 gives message structures, while each scheme and bank determines valid states and actions.
I would encode those rules in a reviewed state machine.

[tag ⑥]
If an external response is missing, we mark the outcome unknown.
We do not release a hold or retry blindly.
The bank's scheme specialists define the permitted status-inquiry path; a pacs.028 may be part of that path in a relevant flow.
Engineering then makes the inquiry idempotent, observable, and timed.

[tag ⑦]
After an external acceptance, a correction may require a recall or investigation.
A camt.056 may be relevant, but eligibility, timing, and outcome depend on the scheme and case.
The bank defines that policy.
The platform records every request and response without promising the funds will return.

[tag ⑧]
The engineering goal is a visible state, a stable tracking ID, and a complete audit trail for each exception.
Operations owns the procedure and the human decision points.

[validation plan]
A scheme simulator can exercise acceptance, rejection, timeout, and inquiry paths.
It is proof of state handling, not proof of scheme certification.

===== Slide 6 · Consistency Without Two-Phase Commit  (14:00 – 16:35) =====

[tag ①]
Now we look at the execution detail.
We cannot use one atomic transaction across Kafka, a service database, a legacy system, and an external party.
We must design for partial failure.

[tag ②]
A payment orchestrator can own the state machine and record each decision.
This diagram shows one possible path: initiated, screened, funds reserved, submitted, accepted, settled.
The bank must confirm the exact states, names, and transitions.

[tag ③]
For a confirmed rejection, the bank may authorise release of the hold.
That action becomes a new recorded event; history is never deleted.
The rule must account for timing and any external response still in flight.

[tag ④]
The dotted line marks a proposed commitment boundary.
Before it, some internal steps may be reversible.
After it, correction may require a separate scheme process.
Bank operations and scheme experts must define the real boundary; it cannot be inferred from a generic diagram.

[tag ⑤]
A timeout means unknown, not failed.
We hold a safe state and use the bank-approved inquiry process before releasing funds or resubmitting.

[tag ⑥]
At the bottom is a transferable engineering failure mode: the dual write.
If a service writes its database and Kafka separately, a crash between them can leave missing or misleading events.
I have seen this class of problem in large systems beyond banking.

[tag ⑦]
A transactional outbox stores the state change and an event record in one database transaction.
Debezium can publish that record to Kafka.
We still need monitoring for lag and failures, and a clear replay procedure.

[tag ⑧]
The same event can arrive more than once.
Consumers use stable IDs and idempotent writes so retries do not create another business effect.
We test this under crashes and replay; saying "exactly once" without the business boundary would overstate the guarantee.

[validation plan]
A proposed timeout test would inspect the event trail and check that no unsafe release occurs while status is unknown.

===== Slide 7 · AI Drafts. Experts Approve. Tests Enforce.  (16:35 – 18:40) =====

[tag ①]
Where can AI help?
In a bank-approved environment, it can draft a candidate rules catalog from code and documents.
It cannot know every hidden rule, or decide what a rule means to the bank.

[tag ②]
Each candidate links to a source line and a test.
Mainframe and business experts check the meaning, resolve gaps, and approve it.
A citation proves where text came from; it does not prove the interpretation.

[tag ③]
AI can then draft interface contracts and architecture decision records, or ADRs.
Engineering validates compatibility and failure behavior.
Bank owners validate the business meaning of every field and state.

[tag ④]
AI can help write Java services, but the value is in enforced design rules.
In the proposed reference build, business code would publish through the outbox, and an unhandled event type would fail the build.
These are engineering controls to implement, not claims about running code.

[tag ⑤]
We would document those constraints for coding agents, and ArchUnit tests could check them automatically.
Reviewers would inspect generated code for correctness and security.

[tag ⑥]
The proposed pipeline would block a merge after a failed architecture check.
That makes the design testable.
Production release would still need the bank's approvals, operational readiness, and evidence.

[tag ⑦]
The proposed proof is specific: send the same request twice in a reference environment, then inspect the response, database, and event stream.
If the result matches the contract under retries and crashes, we have evidence for that boundary.
A bank would still need load, failure, integration, and scheme tests.

===== Slide 8 · Trade-offs & Takeaways  (18:40 – 20:40) =====

[tag ①]
Every architecture has trade-offs.
First, eventual consistency.
Different systems may show different states for a time.
The bank must define what the customer sees, what can be accepted, and how status is tracked.
A status API helps; the identifier and message rules depend on the payment flow.

[tag ②]
Second, operational complexity.
Kafka, CDC, and a saga add failure modes.
They need a funded team, runbooks, monitoring, and on-call capability.
If those cannot be sustained, choose a simpler design.

[tag ③]
Third, orchestration cost.
Managed workflow pricing depends on volume and transitions.
This reference design keeps the state machine in an orchestrator and reserves managed workflows for other flows.
A bank should model both options using its volume, support needs, and team skills.

[tag ④]
Fourth, dual-run cost.
Running old and new paths together can increase spend before it reduces risk or cost.
Leadership needs a baseline, a decommissioning plan, and a measure of capacity actually removed.

[validation plan]
A reference build could support design review through code and tests.
Any future results should state the exact conditions and limits.

[tag ⑤]
Three takeaways.
One: treat the ledger cutover as a bank-owned decision that needs evidence and rehearsed recovery.

[tag ⑥]
Two: define idempotency with business owners, then enforce it in contracts, state, and tests.

[tag ⑦]
Three: use AI to draft, while named experts approve meaning and the pipeline checks implementation.

The method is to make one decision, prove it, and operate it safely before scaling further.
