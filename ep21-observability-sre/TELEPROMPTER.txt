===== Slide 1 · The 3 a.m. Question  (00:00 – 02:05) =====

[tag ①]
The reference roadmap has reached operations.
This is not a bank platform I have run; it is a way to examine the execution problem.
Imagine a customer asking at three in the morning: "I sent five hundred euros an hour ago.
Where is it?"
The bank defines the correct status and communication; engineering must make the evidence findable.

[tag ②]
Without joined telemetry, an operator may search the app, gateway, Kafka, mainframe and external connection separately.
The eight teams and three-hour delay on the diagram are illustrative.
The engineering question is how to shorten that search while protecting customer data.

[tag ③]
A shared correlation key lets us search once.
Where a scheme supplies a UETR, we link it to our internal payment and trace IDs.
Other rails may use different identifiers, so the bank's integration team defines the mapping.

[tag ④]
The trace can show which internal steps completed, which external handoff was attempted, and where status is still uncertain.
Customer care should use only a bank-approved status message.
A trace does not prove that another bank has received or settled the payment.

[tag ⑤]
The proposed outcome is faster investigation, earlier detection, a usable incident trail and cost attribution.
The twenty-second answer on the slide is a design target; it needs an incident drill and measured search time before anyone can claim it.

[validation plan]
A reference observability harness could join a synthetic payment ID across traces, metrics, logs and events.
That would test the correlation design; it would not prove production performance.

===== Slide 2 · Four Signals, One Key  (02:05 – 03:55) =====

[tag ①]
In this reference architecture, telemetry comes from the proposed Kubernetes services, Kafka, the mainframe adapter, the external gateway and the API edge.
The actual bank estate would determine the integration points.

[tag ②]
OpenTelemetry gives these components a common way to emit signals.
Collectors can enrich records with a safe correlation token and retain slow or failed traces.
The collector design needs scale, failure isolation and data-governance tests; using an open format does not remove all vendor dependencies.

[tag ③]
We also minimize and mask sensitive data before storage, then test the filters.
A correlation ID should not reveal an account number or customer name.

[tag ④]
The collector produces four signals.
Logs tell us what happened, in words.
Metrics tell us how much and how fast.
Traces follow one payment, step by step.

[tag ⑤]
A fourth signal in this design is domain events, from Kafka.
Their names and meanings come from bank-approved business states; a technical event alone must not be presented as settlement proof.

[tag ⑥]
The engineering contract links the external reference, where one exists, to the internal payment ID and trace ID.
We test for missing, reused and delayed identifiers as well as the happy path.

[tag ⑦]
The target is to navigate from any approved identifier to the relevant signals.
AI may flag unusual patterns, but a person verifies the source records before acting.

===== Slide 3 · Follow One Payment  (03:55 – 05:45) =====

[tag ①]
Let's inspect one synthetic trace, not a real customer's payment.
The diagram uses a two-hundred-and-fifty-euro transfer and a linked reference ID to show the method.

[tag ②]
Each bar is a span, one timed technical step.
The fourteen, twenty-two, twenty-eight and one-hundred-and-thirty-eight millisecond values are sample timings.
In a real bank, we would measure them under representative load.

[tag ③]
The red line is an illustrative internal latency budget of five hundred milliseconds.
The sample uses four hundred and sixty-one.
That passes this example budget; it is not a production benchmark or a promise to customers.

[tag ④]
The orange span is sixty-six milliseconds above its example budget.
This is how a trace directs investigation toward a dependency, but one trace is not enough to diagnose a systemic bottleneck.

[tag ⑤]
The external round trip in this sample is about one point one seconds.
We can measure the boundary we observe, but need partner or scheme evidence to explain what happened beyond it.

[tag ⑥]
Below, a business-state timeline maps technical events to bank-approved meanings.
A "settled" state must come from the authoritative source defined by the bank, not merely from a successful API call.

[tag ⑦]
AI could compare many traces and suggest that funds holds became slower after a particular time.
The four-percent figure is illustrative.
An engineer checks the sample size, dependency metrics and change history before confirming cause.

===== Slide 4 · SLOs and Error Budgets  (05:45 – 07:40) =====

[tag ①]
The bank's NFRs become measurable SLOs.
An SLI is the observed signal, such as the share of requests accepted within a time budget.
An SLO is the approved target over a window.
The ninety-nine-percent and thirty-day figures here are examples; product and operations owners set them against actual customer commitments.

[tag ②]
Correctness needs separate controls.
A suspected duplicate debit or payment without a known status triggers investigation immediately.
We do not treat a money-integrity invariant as an ordinary error budget.

[tag ③]
The chart on the right is sample data for a thirty-day error budget.
It shows thirty-eight percent remaining, as an example of how a team would discuss reliability risk.

[tag ④]
In the sample, one incident and one release consume most of that budget.
The point is to connect operational events to an explicit release decision, not to claim these incidents occurred.

[tag ⑤]
We watch burn rate, the speed at which the budget is spent.
The page threshold on the diagram is illustrative.
SRE and product owners tune it against actual traffic, false alarms and customer impact.

[tag ⑥]
A slower burn may become a planned squad task rather than an overnight page, if the bank's incident policy allows it.

[tag ⑦]
Product, SRE and risk owners agree a policy for slowing releases when reliability degrades.
The fifty-percent and zero-budget actions shown are example gates; the bank decides them before a crisis.

===== Slide 5 · Alerts That Wake the Right Person  (07:40 – 09:15) =====

[tag ①]
Too many alerts are as bad as none.
The diagram's two billion daily data points are a scale assumption, not a measured platform result.
The design goal is to help an on-call engineer see customer impact quickly.

[tag ②]
The sample keeps most warnings on dashboards and sends only high-impact conditions to a person.
The exact count of pages must be tuned from observed incidents.

[tag ③]
A strong default is to page on customer-impact symptoms: failures, long delays or missing outcomes.
CPU and pod restarts still matter for diagnosis.
A dependency warning can also deserve a page when it predicts imminent harm; the runbook defines that boundary.

[tag ④]
In this example, fraud latency and stuck-payment alerts rise together.
AI could group them into one candidate incident and suggest a likely connection.
It cannot establish root cause from timing alone.

[tag ⑤]
The ownership map routes the page to the service team, with an escalation path when several teams or external partners are involved.

[tag ⑥]
Every page should have an owner, a tested runbook and a clear action.
We can verify that rule with game days and on-call feedback.

===== Slide 6 · Runbooks and On-Call, with AI  (09:15 – 10:55) =====

[tag ①]
At three in the morning, an engineer needs a runbook, not a blank page.
This example starts with internal status, partner status and trace evidence.
Customer messages, inquiry types and update frequency must follow the bank's approved process.
Compliance determines whether a regulatory incident record is required.

[tag ②]
The orange line warns against an untracked manual re-send.
Idempotent retries and ledger reconciliation reduce duplicate risk, but no architect should promise that a retry key alone makes double charging impossible.

[tag ③]
AI could summarize the incident, link source records and suggest a runbook.
The engineer checks customer-impact counts and time ranges before using them.

[tag ④]
It may draft a status update, but the bank's communication owner approves external wording.

[tag ⑤]
In this design, AI has no authority to move money, issue refunds or change production controls.

[tag ⑥]
Some technical remediation, such as restarting a stateless pod, may be automated after testing.
Adding capacity needs limits.
Changing fraud mode is a bank risk decision and should never be treated as a generic safe switch.

[tag ⑦]
A named person authorizes money-impacting actions, cross-region failover and external communications under the bank's policy.
The owning squads participate in on-call and the recovery drills.

===== Slide 7 · Dashboards for Every Audience  (10:55 – 12:00) =====

[tag ①]
Different owners need different views built from reconciled source data.
A leadership view could show volume, straight-through rate, SLOs and measured legacy workload removed.

[tag ②]
Operations needs a near-live view of flow, delayed outcomes, partner connectivity and open incidents.
The thirty-second threshold shown is a sample policy.

[tag ③]
Each squad tracks p99 latency against an approved budget, error-budget use and recent deployments.

[tag ④]
Risk and Audit need incident evidence, applicable reporting status and recovery-drill results.
Compliance determines which rules apply.

[tag ⑤]
A regular SLO review asks what is at risk, what to fix next and which repeated manual work can be automated safely.
